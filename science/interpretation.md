# Interpretation for quantum training and physical resources

A small training loss is useful only when its relationship to the intended test error is understood. For a learned quantum operation, that relationship depends on the input geometry and on which physical deviations the hypothesis class permits. Exact agreement on a menu does not determine the precision of a finite-loss certificate.

The results here make this relationship explicit for a unitary target, a CPTP hypothesis, pure training inputs, and Haar-average gate infidelity. They give a deterministic transfer from the true mean training loss to test risk, followed by a statistical analysis of how many fresh examples are needed to certify that transfer. The [model](model.md) fixes both tasks.

## 1. What the result says about learning

Suppose a training procedure outputs a fixed channel. Its true mean survival loss on a prescribed ensemble is $`\epsilon_S`$. If the ensemble has positive variance gap, the [robustness theorem](robustness.md) gives

```math
R\le C_S\epsilon_S,
\qquad
C_S=\frac1{(d+1)\mu_S}.
```

This inequality holds for every CPTP hypothesis, independently of the optimizer, parametrization, or trajectory that produced it. It therefore identifies which training objectives can control the chosen test risk throughout that hypothesis class.

The theorem does not determine how a learner reaches a small loss. It does not analyze gradient concentration, optimization time, the amount of data needed to learn an unknown target, or the gate complexity of the learned operation. It also concerns the true ensemble loss. Estimating a loss after selecting the hypothesis from the same observations requires an additional statistical argument. The certification theorem uses fresh data after the hypothesis is fixed.

The test distribution is Haar measure on the full Hilbert space. For a register of qubits, it includes entangled states. A certificate for this average is different from a worst-case input guarantee or a diamond-distance guarantee. Those quantities require their own conversions and resource accounting.

## 2. Why exact identification can be poorly conditioned

The known connected-spanning criterion says when a pure-state menu identifies a unitary exactly among all CPTP channels. Its zero-loss conclusion is strong: no different channel can pass every probe with certainty. Its nonzero-loss stability depends on a finer operator property.

A unitary perturbation is generated by a Hermitian direction. A general channel also has dissipative directions, represented by possibly nonnormal jump operators. A nonscalar operator can have every training vector as a right eigenvector, even if those vectors span and overlap. Such a direction has zero first-order survival loss on each ray. A physical CPTP perturbation built from it can nevertheless have first-order Haar risk.

The simplest [qubit example](probe_geometry.md) uses $`|0\rangle`$ and $`|+\rangle`$. There is an explicit channel for which

```math
\epsilon_S=\frac{(1-\sqrt{1-2t})^2}{8},
\qquad
R=\sqrt{2\epsilon_S}-\frac23\epsilon_S.
```

Both probes identify the identity exactly. Along this family, however, the training signal is quadratic in the noise parameter while the test risk is linear. Arbitrarily accurate knowledge of an almost-zero loss must resolve that weaker signal.

Adding $`|1\rangle`$ removes the nonscalar common right-eigenoperator direction. Uniform weights give the sharp linear coefficient $`2+\sqrt2`$. Optimizing the three weights to $`(3/10,2/5,3/10)`$ improves it to $`10/3`$. The physical content is a change in sensitivity to allowed dissipative errors.

Zero first-order survival loss does not mean the entire output state is stationary. The distinction from the established stationary-state conditions for quantum Markov dynamics is explained in [Related work](../references/RELATED_WORK.md#kraus-et-al-2008).

## 3. The channel prior changes the answer

If the hypothesis is promised to be unital, then every connected spanning menu has a sharp linear coefficient governed by a commutator gap $`\gamma_S`$:

```math
R\le\frac{\epsilon_S}{(d+1)\gamma_S}.
```

The coefficient is approached already by small unitary perturbations. Thus a training analysis that restricts the hypothesis to unitaries can miss a precision obstruction caused by dissipative mismatch in an actual implementation.

A quantitative version uses

```math
u(\mathcal E)=
\frac12\left\|\mathcal E(I/d)-I/d\right\|_1.
```

For a connected spanning ensemble in the singular regime $`\mu_S=0`$, the sharp joint small-loss frontier is

```math
\sup_{\epsilon_S\le\varepsilon,\ u(\mathcal E)\le\eta}R
=\Theta_S\!\left(\varepsilon+\min\{\eta,\sqrt\varepsilon\}\right).
```

The constants keep the ensemble and dimension fixed. Exact unitality gives the linear regime. A nonunitality allowance of order $`\varepsilon`$ also preserves linear order; a fixed positive allowance eventually admits the square-root obstruction as $`\varepsilon`$ decreases. The [nonunitality proof](nonunitality.md) supplies the global inequality and the matching physical families.

Here $`u\le\eta`$ is a physical assumption or an independently justified bound. It is not a free additional observable supplied by the original training menu. Establishing such a promise experimentally can itself require inputs, measurements, and repetitions.

## 4. A small menu can carry a large coefficient

For unrestricted channels on a $`d`$-dimensional system, $`d`$ linearly independent pure probes always leave nonscalar common right eigenoperators. A suitably related $`(d+1)`$st ray can repair the exponent. The best possible coefficient at that support size is still

```math
C_S\ge\frac{d^2}{d+1}.
```

More generally, writing $`N=d+k\le d^2`$, the [support theorem](optimal_design.md) gives

```math
C_S\ge\frac{d(d+k-1)}{(d+1)k}.
```

For $`k\ll d`$, this is of order $`d/k`$. One extra setting can therefore change the asymptotic precision exponent while leaving a coefficient that grows with dimension. Achieving a dimension-independent coefficient requires order $`d`$ additional support rays. Two mutually unbiased bases provide an established constant-order benchmark; the simplex attains the minimal-support optimum. These constructions and their prior attribution are recorded in [Related work](../references/RELATED_WORK.md).

The bound concerns all weighted ensembles, including unbalanced ones. Its equality condition is a uniformly weighted equiangular tight frame, when one exists. At and above $`d^2`$ support points, a weighted projective 2-design attains the universal coefficient $`C_S=1`$ when available.

Support size counts distinct allowable inputs. It does not force an experiment to visit every input. A verifier may sample a large menu and stop after relatively few repetitions. Conversely, a short menu can require many repetitions to resolve its weak error signal.

Preparation complexity is separate again. The known qubit simplex has efficient product-state preparation despite having $`d+1`$ rays. A large support count alone is therefore not a lower bound on circuit depth. The complete finite-error optimum and the exact set of dimensions and cardinalities supporting ETFs remain outside the cardinality theorem.

## 5. Why the geometry becomes an optimal sampling law

An ideal survival test has a useful boundary behavior: the exact target always passes. If every bad channel with $`R\ge\delta`$ must fail with probability at least $`m(\delta)`$, independent tests need order

```math
m(\delta)^{-1}\log(1/\alpha)
```

uses to exclude that alternative with decision error at most $`\alpha`$. Linear robustness supplies $`m(\delta)`$ of order $`\delta`$. A singular connected menu supplies order $`\delta^2`$.

An upper bound from one test would not establish optimality. The [certification proof](certification.md) supplies alternatives whose outputs remain close to the ideal outputs for **every** allowed ray. A classical-to-quantum fidelity converse then applies to arbitrary adaptive probe selection, retained quantum memory, and joint measurements on all collected outputs. These more general readouts cannot remove the same weak-signal obstruction under the stated input contract.

For a fixed finite menu and dimension, let $`\mu_*`$ be the maximum variance gap over sampling weights. With ideal target-output projectors, the optimal worst-case call cap satisfies

```math
\lim_{\substack{\delta\to0\\\alpha\to0}}
\frac{\delta T_{\rm opt}(\delta,\alpha)}{\log(1/\alpha)}
=\frac1{(d+1)\mu_*}
\qquad(\mu_*>0).
```

No relation between the two rates of convergence is needed. At fixed confidence and with arbitrary two-sided errors, the proof gives a finite bracket instead of asserting the same exact coefficient. Perfect completeness makes the coefficient exact at fixed confidence as well. The general theorem keeps dimension fixed; a sufficient onset condition is $`\delta\ll\mu_* /(d+1)`$.

The fresh input is chosen through a classical label and is conditionally independent of retained verifier memory. An allowed ray may itself be entangled across the system. Entangling each new channel input with memory, coherently choosing the probe label, passing previous outputs through the channel again, or observing its environment changes the access model. The lower bound does not supply conclusions for those additional resources.

Fresh trusted pure target copies give an alternative implementation of the comparison test. One copy and one hypothesis output yield rejection probability $`e_i/2`$ through the antisymmetric test. This preserves the precision exponents with a factor-two sufficient overhead. The exact optimal reference-copy coefficient is a separate question.

The favorable $`\delta^{-1}`$ law also depends on testing near a perfectly passing target. A shrinking acceptance tolerance preserves that order when the separation is proportional to the same small loss scale. A fixed noisy baseline has sampling fluctuations already present at zero additional error. The certification page constructs a depolarizing example that requires order $`\delta^{-2}\log(1/\alpha)`$ even for the best probe geometry.

## 6. A scalable consequence under exact tensor factorization

For an exact tensor product of $`n`$ local qubit channels, with a product-unitary target, the [product-channel theorem](product_channels.md) has constants uniform in register size. For $`0\lt \delta\le1/8`$ and confidence bounded away from $`1/2`$:

| Available global probes | Optimal global examples |
|---|---|
| $`\lvert0\rangle^{\otimes n}`$ and $`\lvert+\rangle^{\otimes n}`$ | $`\Theta(n\delta^{-2}\log(1/\alpha))`$ |
| Those two probes plus $`\lvert1\rangle^{\otimes n}`$ | $`\Theta(\delta^{-1}\log(1/\alpha))`$ |

The two-direction lower bound still holds if every mixed tensor string from local $`|0\rangle`$ and $`|+\rangle`$ is allowed. Thus its cost is not caused by withholding those mixed strings.

For the three uniform global strings, weights $`(3/10,2/5,3/10)`$ give the exact global coefficient $`5D/(D+1)`$, where $`D=2^n`$. At fixed $`n`$, the same coefficient is optimal in the joint small-error, high-confidence sampling limit.

Each global example prepares and measures $`n`$ qubits and invokes all $`n`$ local channels. Counting local channel uses therefore multiplies the displayed sample costs by $`n`$. The constructive tests use order $`n`$ ideal preparation and measurement primitives per example; precision-dependent compilation and calibration costs require separate specification.

Exact factorization is the central promise. Independent-looking marginal channels, approximate factorization, and correlated mixtures of product channels are different conditions. The three-setting theorem does not imply the same guarantee for arbitrary correlated noise.

There are already dimension-independent process-fidelity bounds for arbitrary channels using a full product-SIC input ensemble, as proved in [Mayer's 0-fidelity note](../references/RELATED_WORK.md#mayer-2021). The present product application exchanges a stronger channel promise for a much smaller global menu and obtains matching adaptive sample laws within that promise.

## 7. What is established and what remains open

The proof pages establish the optimal global linear coefficient, its necessary and sufficient geometric criterion, the universal support bound and its equality conditions, the sharp unital coefficient and nonunital crossover, and the adaptive certification consequences. The product application supplies a separately uniform family in which one extra probe changes both the precision exponent and register-size dependence.

The known exact-identification theorem, square-root upper bound, frame constructions, and fidelity converse framework retain their attribution in [Related work](../references/RELATED_WORK.md). The work here develops the missing geometric necessity, sharp constructions, universal optimization, and resulting operational statements within those foundations.

Several extensions require additional work: the complete finite-error curve for arbitrary menus; certification with imperfect or untrusted references; approximate or correlated product structure; and joint dimension-and-precision limits beyond the proved product family. These questions do not follow from the present inequalities by dropping their assumptions.

The [finite checks](../verification/README.md) reproduce the concrete channels, matrices, and scalar formulas. Their role is to corroborate the displayed constructions. The universal and optimality claims rest on the analytical proofs.
